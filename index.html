Clue Academy

A five-minute reading lesson. One feature is never reliable proof of authorship. Human writers may sound formal, and AI may include personal details that it invented.

Clue 1 — Personal voice

Ask: Is there a meaningful viewpoint, memory, or small observation? An AI can imitate a memory, so check rather than assume.

Clue 2 — Repetition

Find repeated openings, phrases, or predictable transitions. Repetition also occurs in student essays.

Clue 3 — Sentence variety

Compare short and long sentences. Both humans and AI can vary their sentences.

Clue 4 — Specificity and accuracy

Underline concrete examples. A precise claim is not automatically a true claim; verify important facts separately.

Clue 5 — Multiple explanations

Ask what else could explain the pattern: editing, a writing prompt, English proficiency, genre, or an AI tool.

Investigator's rule: Text alone rarely proves authorship. Source records, drafts, teacher observation, and prompt logs are stronger evidence.

How to interpret results

Accuracy = correct guesses ÷ total passages × 100. Learning gain = post-test accuracy − pre-test accuracy in percentage points. Report the number of participants and the actual source of every passage. A 50% score is the expected average for random guessing in this balanced two-choice exercise, not a fixed standard for every real-world test.

Do not claim that a higher demo score shows actual ability to identify AI. Do not collect student names, grades, or other personal information in the anonymous-code field.

Classroom Study Guide

  1. Collect parental/school permission as appropriate and anonymise any student writing.
  2. Prepare 8 verified pre-test passages and 8 different verified post-test passages, each with 4 human and 4 AI items. Match topics, word lengths, genres, and reading levels as closely as possible.
  3. Enter the passages and source evidence in Teacher Studio. Teacher evidence notes remain in the local device's corpus and JSON export, not in CSV attempt records.
  4. Give each participant an anonymous code. Students complete the pre-test without seeing answers along the way.
  5. Teach the five clues, including their limitations. Participants check the completion box with their code.
  6. Students take the post-test. Export data and compare paired results for the same anonymous codes.
  7. Report the sample size, conditions, uncertain classifications, average confidence, and limitations. Never invent collected data.

What happens in a class of 24 students?

Use the same corpus for everyone, issue 24 anonymous codes, and allow each learner to take a pre- and post-test. On a shared device, results accumulate locally. On separate devices, export each device's CSV and combine them in a spreadsheet using code + phase. The website does not sync between devices.

Can students use automatic AI detection as proof?

No. This activity examines reading judgments, not disciplinary accusations. A wrong guess should lead to discussion of evidence and uncertainty, never a penalty for a classmate.

How can a teacher avoid a biased test?

Keep texts similar in genre, topic, word count, proofreading, formatting, and English difficulty. Do not deliberately make AI items polished and human items full of mistakes. Shuffle item order, collect a pilot, and keep source labels hidden until the test ends.
